## Light and location

Strawberries need plenty of sun, ideally about 6 hours of direct sunlight a day, for heavy flowering and sweet fruit. In too little light the plants grow leaves but set few berries with weak flavor. In hot climates, morning sun with light afternoon shade keeps the plants from overheating.

This is a low-growing plant that stays close to the ground, so it needs no support. On a bed, mulch around the plants with straw or plastic so the fruit does not touch the soil and moisture is retained. Containers or vertical planters work well too, but use a pot wide enough for the spreading root system.

## Watering

Strawberries have shallow roots and need evenly moist soil from flowering until the fruit ripens. Water when the top layer begins to dry; dry soil gives small, tart berries, while soil kept soggy for long can rot the roots. Check the soil with your hand before watering.

Avoid wetting the leaves and fruit, especially late in the day, since fruit that stays damp is prone to rot. Water at the base or use drip irrigation close to the ground. In containers the soil dries faster and needs checking more often.

## Soil

Choose loose, well-drained soil rich in organic matter. Poor drainage is a common cause of strawberries wilting or dying from root rot. Work compost into the bed before planting; on heavy soil, raise the bed so water drains away quickly.

## Feeding

Strawberries need steady nutrition but cannot handle concentrated fertilizer. Work compost or well-rotted manure into the soil before planting, then feed lightly once flowering begins. During fruiting, favor a fertilizer with more potassium than nitrogen for firm, sweet berries.

Watch the leaves before adding more. Pale leaves and slow growth may call for extra nutrition; dark green, vigorous plants need none. Keep fertilizer away from the crown and do not feed while the soil is dry.

## Sowing and propagation

Strawberries are usually planted as rooted plants rather than grown from seed. When planting, set the crown at exactly soil level: the crown sits on the surface, not buried deeply and not left exposed above the soil. Wrong planting depth is the most common reason new plants die.

As the plant matures it sends out runners bearing small plantlets. To propagate, press a plantlet on a runner into moist soil or a small pot until it roots, then cut it free from the mother plant once it is established. If you do not need more plants, remove the runners so the mother plant puts energy into fruit.

## Ongoing care and harvest

Keep the planting area clean and open. Mulching with straw or dry material around the plants keeps the fruit off the soil, holds moisture, and suppresses weeds. Remove old or diseased leaves to keep the canopy airy, especially after harvest.

Fruit is ripe when it turns an even red (or the color typical of the variety) and smells sweet. Pick with a short piece of stem attached, handling the berries gently since they bruise easily. Harvest regularly once the season starts; berries left too long on the plant rot and attract pests. Pick in the early morning while the fruit is still cool.

## Common pests and diseases

[Snails and slugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/slugs_snails) commonly feed on ripe fruit and young leaves in damp spots. Check around the base and under the leaves in the early morning or evening; keep the area clean and clear of material lying on the ground that gives them shelter. [Spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) may appear in hot, dry weather, yellowing the leaves and leaving fine webbing.

[Botrytis (gray mold)](richfarm://pests-diseases/botrytis_gray_mold) is a common strawberry disease: fruit softens, discolors, then becomes covered in gray mold, especially when the berries stay damp and the canopy is dense. Pick ripe fruit often, remove rotting berries, and keep the canopy open. If the plant wilts while the soil is still wet, check the drainage first; roots kept waterlogged can rot and need the correct cause addressed before treatment.

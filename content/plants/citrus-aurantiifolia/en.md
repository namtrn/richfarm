## Light and location

Lime trees are sun-loving and need many hours of direct sunlight a day to flower and produce sweet fruit. A full-sun spot keeps the tree healthy and less prone to pests; too much shade makes it grow leggy with little fruit. In cooler climates, plant in a sheltered spot that catches the most sun.

This is a small woody tree or bush with thorns on the branches. It needs no support, but give it room to spread its canopy. Container growing works well as long as the pot is large and the location is sunny; potted trees need more attention to water and nutrition.

## Watering

Limes cannot tolerate waterlogged roots: soil kept soggy for long is a common cause of yellowing, dropping leaves. Water when the top layer begins to dry, soak around the base, then let the soil drain before the next watering. In rainy regions, make sure water drains away quickly from the base.

Potted trees dry faster than garden soil, but they can also become waterlogged if the pot drains poorly. Check the drainage holes regularly and never leave the pot standing in water. When watering, avoid repeatedly wetting the trunk and foliage to limit disease.

## Soil

Choose well-drained, loose, fertile soil. Heavy soil that holds water needs to be improved with compost before planting. In containers, use a citrus or well-draining potting mix; planting on a raised mound helps water drain away from the trunk.

## Feeding

Citrus trees need regular but moderate feeding. Apply well-rotted organic fertilizer or a fertilizer formulated for citrus as directed, splitting it into several applications through the year rather than one large dose. Flowering and fruit development are the times of highest demand.

Watch the leaves before adding more. Pale leaves between the veins can point to a nutrient problem, but they can also result from waterlogged roots or compacted soil, so check the growing conditions before increasing fertilizer. Keep fertilizer away from the trunk and do not feed while the soil is dry.

## Sowing and propagation

Limes are usually planted as grafted or layered trees so they keep the variety's traits and fruit sooner than seed-grown plants. Seedlings can grow, but they take years to fruit and the quality is unpredictable.

Set the tree at the same depth it grew in its pot, without burying the trunk too deep. Water well after planting and keep the soil evenly moist for the first few weeks. If growing in a container, move up to larger pots as the tree grows and refresh the soil after a few years when the old mix is depleted.

## Ongoing care and harvest

A lime is a long-lived tree that needs care by stage. Prune out dry, diseased, or inward-growing branches to keep the canopy open so sunlight and air reach the inside. For potted trees, repot into a larger container once the roots fill the pot.

Fruit develops slowly and can take many months from flowering to ripeness. The fruit is ready when it reaches full size, the rind turns the typical green-yellow color, and it smells fragrant. Cut the fruit with scissors, leaving the stem attached, so the branch is not damaged. In frost-prone areas, shelter the tree or move the pot somewhere warm in winter.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young shoots, curling and distorting new leaves. Check the new growth regularly; a light infestation can be washed off or removed by hand. [Mealybugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/mealybugs) and [spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) also affect citrus, especially in dry conditions or on potted trees.

Prolonged leaf yellowing with wilting branches while the soil stays wet points to drainage problems first, since [root rot](richfarm://pests-diseases/root_rot) from waterlogging is common in citrus. If the leaves develop many spreading spots, this may be [leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot); remove affected leaves and keep the canopy open. When disease spreads widely or several trees show the same symptoms, identify the cause before using any product.

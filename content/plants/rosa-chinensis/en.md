## Light and location

Roses need plenty of sun, at least about 6 hours of direct sunlight a day, to flower heavily and stay healthy. An open, airy spot lets the leaves dry quickly after rain and limits leaf spot and powdery mildew. A dark, damp location or one exposed to constant wind weakens the plant and reduces flowering.

Most commonly grown roses are upright, thorny shrubs that need no support. Climbing types are the exception and need a trellis or fence to grow upward; check whether the variety is a bush or a climber before choosing a spot. Leave space between plants so the canopy stays open and the bushes do not shade one another.

## Watering

Soak the root zone when the top layer of soil begins to dry, then let the soil drain before the next watering. Deep, infrequent watering encourages deeper roots better than light daily watering. Potted roses dry faster than garden soil and need checking more often.

Avoid wetting the leaves, especially late in the day, since foliage that stays damp overnight invites leaf spot. Direct the water to the base, using a gentle hose close to the ground. In hot weather the plant needs steadier water; after heavy rain, reduce or skip watering.

## Soil

Choose loose, fertile soil with good drainage. Work compost or well-rotted manure into the bed before planting so the roots establish quickly. Poorly drained soil weakens the roots and invites disease; on heavy soil, plant on a raised bed.

## Feeding

Roses bloom in repeated flushes and need steady nutrition. Apply well-rotted organic fertilizer or a rose-specific product as directed, split into several applications over the growing season. Bud formation and flowering are the periods of highest demand.

Watch the leaves before adding more. Pale leaves and sparse blooms can point to a nutrient shortage, but they can also result from too little sun or waterlogged roots. Keep fertilizer away from the stem and do not feed while the soil is dry; water lightly after feeding so it dissolves and reaches the roots.

## Sowing and propagation

Roses are usually planted as grafted or rooted cutting plants. Set the plant at the same depth it grew in its pot, without burying the graft union if there is one. Early in the growing season, when the weather is mild, is the best planting time.

To propagate, stem cuttings are the common method: take a firm, healthy shoot, cut it at an angle, strip most of the leaves, and place it in moist growing medium until roots form. Cutting-grown plants usually keep the traits of the mother plant.

## Ongoing care and harvest

Pruning is the most important part of rose care. Remove dry, weak, or inward-growing branches and spent flowers so the plant channels energy into new growth. Cut just above a leaf bud that faces outward, so the new shoot grows away from the center. On repeat-flowering varieties, regular deadheading encourages the next flush.

Cut roses for a vase when the bud is just opening, in the early morning while the plant is full of water. Use clean scissors, cut the stem at an angle, and put it in water right away. Frequent cutting also keeps the bush tidy and promotes even blooming.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on buds, young shoots, and the undersides of young leaves, distorting buds and curling foliage. Check the buds and tips regularly; a light infestation can be washed off or removed by hand. [Spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) appear in hot, dry weather, yellowing the leaves and leaving fine webbing.

[Leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot) is a common rose disease, showing black or brown spots that usually start on the lower leaves. Pick up fallen leaves, remove affected ones, and avoid wetting the foliage. In damp weather, [powdery mildew](richfarm://pests-diseases/powdery_mildew) can coat young leaves and buds with a white powder; cut out the affected parts and keep the canopy open. If disease spreads widely, identify the cause before using any product.

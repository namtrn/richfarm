## Light and location

Zucchini is a sun-loving plant that needs at least 6 hours of direct sunlight a day to flower and set fruit. A sunny spot keeps the plant healthy and limits powdery mildew. In hot climates, grow it during the cooler part of the year or choose an airy location.

The plant grows as a spreading bush and does not climb, so it needs no trellis. Give it plenty of room, since the leaves are large and the plant sprawls; planting too close together makes the canopy dense, poorly ventilated, and prone to disease. Growing in a large container works, as long as the pot is wide enough for the root system.

## Watering

Keep the soil evenly moist, especially while the plant is flowering and fruiting. Water when the top layer begins to dry, soaking around the base. Dry soil makes flowers drop and young fruit stop growing; soil kept soggy for long can rot the roots.

Avoid wetting the leaves, especially late in the day, since the large leaves hold moisture and invite disease. Water at the base or use a gentle hose close to the ground. A thin mulch layer helps the soil hold moisture more steadily.

## Soil

Choose loose, fertile soil with good drainage. Work mature compost or well-rotted manure into the bed before planting. In containers, use a large pot with well-draining soil and clear drainage holes.

## Feeding

Prepare the bed before planting, then feed lightly once flowering begins. During fruit development, favor a fertilizer with more potassium than nitrogen for firm fruit. Watch the leaves before adding more: pale leaves and slow growth despite enough water may signal a nutrient shortage.

Keep concentrated fertilizer away from the stem and do not feed while the soil is dry. Too much nitrogen produces many leaves but few flowers and fruit.

## Sowing and propagation

Zucchini grows quickly from seed and is usually sown directly where it will grow. Sow once the soil has warmed and frost is no longer a risk; seed germinates fast in warm soil. Plant 2-3 seeds per spot and thin to the strongest seedling.

In cool regions, sow after the last frost; in warm climates, sow successive batches to extend the harvest. If starting seedlings indoors, disturb the roots as little as possible when transplanting.

## Ongoing care and harvest

The plant produces separate male and female flowers on the same vine. Female flowers have a small fruit at the base; male flowers have only a stem and stamens. Fruit develops from the female flowers after pollination by insects; if few insects are around, hand-pollinate by transferring pollen from a male to a female flower in the morning.

Fruit tastes best when young, picked at about 15-20 cm long depending on the variety, while the skin is still soft and the seeds have not developed. Left too large, the skin hardens, the seeds enlarge, and the plant stops setting new fruit. Cut the fruit with a knife or scissors, leaving a short piece of stem. Harvest regularly, sometimes daily while the plant is at peak production.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young shoots and leaf undersides, curling leaves and slowing growth. Check the tips and flower buds while watering; a light infestation can be washed off or removed by hand. [Snails and slugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/slugs_snails) may feed on young leaves and fruit lying near the ground, especially in damp spots.

[Powdery mildew](richfarm://pests-diseases/powdery_mildew) appears as a white powdery coating on the upper leaf surface, often when leaves stay damp and the canopy is dense. Remove affected leaves, open up the canopy, and avoid wetting the foliage. If young fruit rots at the flower end while the soil swings between dry and wet, steady the watering rather than reaching for a product.

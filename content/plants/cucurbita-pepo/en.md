Zucchini grows quickly in warm weather and can produce heavily once flowering begins, but its broad canopy and fast-growing fruit need regular attention. Poor pollination can leave young fruit yellowing and collapsing, crowded leaves can stay damp and develop mildew, and oversized fruit can be easy to miss beneath the foliage. Give the plant sun, space, steady moisture, and check it often once harvest starts.

## Give the plant sun, space, and fertile soil

Grow zucchini in a sunny position with about 6-8 hours of direct light when possible. Most garden zucchini forms a broad bush, so leave enough room for mature leaves to spread without completely overlapping neighboring plants.

Use loose, well-drained soil enriched with compost or other well-rotted organic matter. In containers, choose a large pot with reliable drainage rather than a small container that dries out quickly. Avoid pushing excessive leafy growth with heavy nitrogen feeding; once flowering and fruiting begin, use a balanced vegetable fertilizer according to the product directions.

## Watch flowers before assuming there is a problem

Zucchini produces separate male and female flowers on the same plant. Male flowers often appear first, and this alone is not a sign that the plant needs more fertilizer.

If female flowers open but the small fruit behind them repeatedly yellows and stops growing, poor pollination may be involved. Where pollinating insects are scarce, hand-pollination can help: use fresh pollen from an open male flower and gently transfer it to the center of an open female flower in the morning. Do this only while both flowers are fresh and open.

## Keep moisture steady and foliage reasonably dry

Zucchini needs consistent moisture, especially once large leaves and developing fruit increase water demand. Check the soil and water deeply around the root zone when it begins to dry rather than following a rigid calendar.

Water at soil level where practical. This reduces leaf wetness and soil splash. A light mulch can help slow moisture loss, but keep the crown clear and make sure the soil still drains freely.

Do not remove healthy leaves on a fixed schedule. Instead, take off leaves that are badly damaged, diseased, or lying on the soil when their removal improves airflow without stripping the plant of useful foliage.

## Harvest while the fruit is still tender

Zucchini is usually best when picked young, before the skin and seeds become tough. Fruit can enlarge quickly in warm weather, so check plants frequently during peak production rather than relying on a fixed number of days after flowering.

Use a clean knife or shears to cut the fruit stem. Regular harvesting keeps oversized fruit from hiding under the canopy and makes it easier to inspect the plant for damaged leaves or pests.

## Distinguish normal leaf markings from disease

Some zucchini cultivars naturally have pale or silvery markings on their leaves. These markings are part of the leaf tissue and should not be confused automatically with fungal growth.

[Powdery mildew](richfarm://pests-diseases/powdery_mildew) usually appears as a white, powdery coating that spreads over the leaf surface. Improve airflow, remove badly affected leaves, and avoid keeping the foliage wet for long periods.

[Downy mildew](richfarm://pests-diseases/downy_mildew) can produce angular yellow areas between leaf veins, often with darker growth on the underside in humid conditions. Remove badly affected material and keep the canopy as dry and open as practical.

## Check common pests under the canopy

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) may gather on young growth and leaf undersides, causing curling and sticky honeydew. Small colonies can often be reduced with water or manual removal.

[Slugs and snails](richfarm://pests-diseases/slugs_snails) can damage seedlings and low fruit, especially where the ground stays damp and cluttered. Check beneath leaves and around the base in the evening or early morning and remove hiding places near the plant.

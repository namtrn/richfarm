Mango is a long-lived tree that needs strong sun and room for an open canopy. An established tree can tolerate a dry period, but waterlogged soil, dense growth, or prolonged rain during flowering can weaken it and reduce fruit set. Choose a permanent site, water deeply by soil condition, and prune after each crop.

## Choose a site and regional season

Mango needs full sun, well-drained ground, and room for branches to spread as the tree grows. In tropical Vietnam, plant young trees during warm weather when the soil can drain; in wet areas, use a mound or drainage furrow. In a temperate part of the United States, reliable outdoor culture is limited to areas without prolonged cold; colder sites need a large container or greenhouse.

A moderate dry period supports flower initiation in tropical areas, but a young tree should not be forced into prolonged wilting. Keep the canopy open so young branches and flower clusters are less likely to be damaged.

## Water deeply and wait for wet soil to drain

After planting, soak the root zone so the tree establishes, then allow the surface to dry slightly before watering again. An established tree in the ground should be checked according to weather rather than a fixed calendar. During prolonged rain, improve drainage; when the soil is dry deep into the root zone and leaves droop in the morning, water slowly around the canopy edge.

Do not concentrate water at the trunk or let it pool beneath the canopy. A thin organic mulch layer steadies the root zone, but leave open space around the trunk.

## Use draining soil and feed according to growth

Deep, loose, well-drained soil supports long-term roots. Mix mature compost while preparing the planting hole, but do not place concentrated fertilizer against young roots. If new leaves are pale or shoots are weak, spread a thin organic layer around the canopy edge and water lightly.

Avoid heavy nitrogen applications just before a desired flowering period, since strong leafy growth can replace flowers. With a packaged fertilizer, measure and apply it according to the label; do not feed dry soil or just before heavy rain.

## Plant a grafted tree and protect the roots

A grafted tree from a clear source usually keeps fruit traits more reliably than a seedling. Choose a healthy plant, set the root ball at the same level it grew before, and do not bury the graft union if one is visible.

Keep grass and competing plants away from the root area while the tree establishes. Seed propagation is possible, but seedlings may differ from the parent in fruiting time, size, or flavor.

## Shape the canopy and harvest at the right stage

Once the tree is established, remove dead branches, inward-crossing growth, and shoots below the graft. After each crop, prune lightly so healthy branches receive light, but do not remove a large share of the canopy at once. A tree that grows beyond safe reach becomes harder to inspect and harvest, so keep its height manageable.

Fruit color varies by variety, so do not judge ripeness by color alone. Watch for full shoulders and aroma; cut the stem with scissors so the branch is not torn. Firm fruit can continue ripening in a clean, airy place.

## Check early for pests and disease

[Thrips](richfarm://pests-diseases/thrips) may leave silvery scars and distort young leaves or flowers. [Mealybugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/mealybugs) form white cottony clusters on shoots and branch joints, while [spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) cause yellow stippling in hot, dry weather. Inspect shoots, flower clusters, and leaf undersides; prune heavy clusters and rinse a light problem.

[Powdery mildew](richfarm://pests-diseases/powdery_mildew) coats young leaves or flower clusters with a powdery layer. [Leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot) produces brown marks that expand across the foliage. Open the canopy, avoid wetting leaves, and identify the cause before using a product.

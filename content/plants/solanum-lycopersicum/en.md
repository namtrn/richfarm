Tomatoes are productive plants, but they respond poorly to sudden swings in moisture, crowded foliage, and weak support. Cracked fruit, blossom-end rot, and leaf diseases often become worse when watering is erratic or the lower canopy stays damp. Give the plant strong light, steady root-zone moisture, and a support system that matches the type you are growing.

## Give tomatoes full sun and support them early

Grow tomatoes where they receive at least about 6 hours of direct sun, with more light generally helping flowering and fruit development when temperatures are suitable.

Install stakes, cages, or a trellis while plants are still small so you do not have to force supports through an established root system later. Tie stems loosely with soft material where needed. Indeterminate tomatoes usually need ongoing support as they continue to grow, while compact determinate types may need less training.

## Water deeply and avoid sharp moisture swings

Check the soil rather than watering only by the calendar. When the upper soil begins to dry, water deeply around the root zone and allow excess water to drain.

Repeated drying followed by heavy watering can contribute to fruit cracking and blossom-end rot. A loose mulch can help slow moisture loss and reduce soil splash onto lower leaves. Water at the soil surface where practical instead of wetting the entire canopy, especially late in the day.

## Use well-drained soil and feed without forcing soft growth

Tomatoes grow best in fertile, well-drained soil with plenty of organic matter. Compost or well-rotted organic material can be incorporated before planting to improve soil structure and provide background fertility.

Use fertilizer according to the soil condition and the product label. Very heavy nitrogen feeding can produce vigorous leafy growth without improving fruiting. Once flowering and fruiting are underway, avoid repeatedly increasing fertilizer just because the plant is large or green.

Container plants need enough soil volume to hold moisture between waterings and must have reliable drainage. Small pots can dry quickly and make moisture swings harder to control.

## Transplant deeply only when the stem is healthy

Tomato stems can form additional roots from buried portions of the stem. When transplanting a healthy, leggy seedling, you can set part of the lower stem below the soil line, removing leaves that would otherwise be buried.

Do not bury damaged, diseased, or waterlogged stem tissue. After transplanting, water the root zone well and keep the plant supported while new roots establish.

## Prune according to growth habit, not by default

Indeterminate tomatoes can be trained to one or a few main stems by removing selected side shoots, especially where space is limited. This can improve access and airflow, but it is a management choice rather than a requirement for every plant.

Determinate tomatoes should not be heavily de-suckered because many flowers and fruits are carried on those side branches. For either type, remove badly diseased leaves and leaves resting on the soil, and avoid stripping so much foliage that fruit becomes exposed to intense sun.

## Harvest according to ripeness and weather

Tomatoes can be picked when they begin to color and allowed to finish ripening indoors, or left longer on the plant when conditions are favorable. If heavy rain is expected after a dry period, harvesting fruit that has already started coloring can reduce the chance of splitting.

Handle fruit gently and cut or detach it at the natural joint without tearing nearby stems.

## Inspect pests and diseases separately

### Pests

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young growth and leaf undersides. Look for curled shoots and sticky honeydew, and reduce small colonies with water or manual removal.

[Caterpillars](richfarm://pests-diseases/caterpillars) may chew leaves or fruit. Frass, missing leaf tissue, or holes in fruit are useful signs to inspect the canopy closely.

[Whiteflies](richfarm://pests-diseases/whiteflies) flutter from disturbed foliage and can leave sticky honeydew behind. Yellow sticky traps can help monitor adults, but the leaves should still be checked for immature stages.

### Diseases

[Early blight](richfarm://pests-diseases/early_blight) often begins on older lower leaves as dark spots that may develop concentric rings. Remove badly affected foliage, reduce soil splash, and keep the lower canopy open.

[Late blight](richfarm://pests-diseases/late_blight) can spread quickly in cool, damp conditions and cause dark, water-soaked lesions on leaves, stems, or fruit. Remove suspect infected material promptly and avoid moving wet foliage between plants.

Do not treat every yellow leaf or wilted plant as the same disease. Compare where symptoms start, how quickly they spread, and whether roots or soil moisture are also involved before choosing a control method.

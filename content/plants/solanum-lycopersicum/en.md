## Light and location

Tomatoes are sun-loving plants that need at least 6 hours of direct sunlight a day for good flowering and fruit set. A full-sun spot keeps the stems compact and helps fruit color evenly. In too little light, plants grow leafy but set fewer fruit and tend to have more disease problems.

Unlike soft-stemmed climbers, tomato stems grow upright but are fairly soft and easily bend under the weight of fruit. Set up stakes, cages, or a simple support at planting time rather than waiting until the plants are tall. As the plant grows, tie the main stem loosely with soft ties and check them regularly so they do not cut into the stem.

## Watering

Give the plants a thorough drink when the top layer of soil begins to dry, then wait for the soil to drain before the next watering. This encourages roots to grow deeper than frequent light watering does. Check the soil with your hand first, since water needs change with the weather and the plant's stage.

Keep soil moisture fairly steady from fruit set to harvest. Soil that swings between very dry and very wet can cause blossom end rot, a fruit disorder linked to uneven calcium uptake. Avoid wetting the foliage, especially late in the day, to limit fungal disease. In containers, soil dries faster than garden beds on hot days and needs more frequent checking.

## Soil

Tomatoes grow best in loose, fertile soil with good drainage. Work mature compost or well-rotted manure into the bed before planting so the plants start with a steady supply of nutrients. Heavy or poorly drained soil weakens the roots and invites disease.

## Feeding

This crop is a heavier feeder than short-season leafy greens, so prepare the bed well and side-dress during growth. Once flowering starts, shift to a fertilizer with more potassium relative to nitrogen to support fruiting rather than pushing leafy growth.

Watch the leaves and growth rate before adding more. Lower leaves turning pale while the plant has enough water may point to a nutrient shortage; very dark leaves and unusually thick stems suggest too much nitrogen. Never pile concentrated fertilizer against the stem, and do not feed while the soil is dry.

## Sowing and propagation

In warm climates, sow seed in late winter or early spring once nights have warmed. In areas with frost, start seed indoors about 6-8 weeks before the last frost date and move seedlings out after the danger has passed. Harden off young plants gradually to sun and wind before planting them in the garden.

Set transplants slightly deeper than they grew in the pot, burying part of the stem below the seed leaves, since this section can form extra roots and anchor the plant. Spacing depends on the type: compact bush types can be planted closer together, while vigorous vining types need more room for air to move through the canopy.

## Ongoing care and harvest

For types that keep growing tall, pinch out the suckers that form in the leaf axils so the plant channels energy into the main stem and fruit trusses. For compact bush types, simply remove weak shoots or old lower leaves to keep the center open. Do this by hand or with clean scissors, taking care not to scrape the stem.

Fruit is approaching ripeness when it begins to change color from green to red, or to the color typical of the variety. You can pick fruit as soon as color starts to show and let it finish ripening in a cool, airy spot, or wait for full color on the plant. Harvest regularly, holding the stem or cutting the fruit with a short piece of stalk so you do not break the branch. Before a long cold or rainy spell, pick fruit that has already begun to color to avoid cracking and rot.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young shoots and leaf undersides, curling leaves and slowing growth. Check the tips when you water; a light infestation can be washed off or removed by hand. [Caterpillars](richfarm://pests-diseases/caterpillars) may chew leaves and bore into fruit; ragged leaf damage or droppings on the plant are a sign to search and remove them by hand.

[Early blight](richfarm://pests-diseases/early_blight) usually starts on the lower leaves as brown spots with concentric rings and moves upward. Remove affected leaves, keep foliage off the soil, and avoid wetting the leaves when watering. [Late blight](richfarm://pests-diseases/late_blight) spreads quickly in cool, damp weather and turns leaves and fruit soft and rotten; catch it early, cut out the affected parts, and keep plants dry overnight. If the disease keeps spreading or many plants are affected at once, identify the cause before using any product, especially on a crop you harvest continuously.

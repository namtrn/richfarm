Guava is a small tree that tolerates many conditions but crops more reliably with sun, draining soil, and pruning after each harvest. It can flower in several flushes, so dry soil or heavy feeding can make fruiting uneven. Judge ripeness by aroma and texture as well as by the variety's skin color.

## Choose sun and the right regional season

Guava needs full sun for sturdy branches and well-flavored fruit. In tropical Vietnam, plant young trees during warm weather and grow them through the year when the soil does not flood. In a temperate part of the United States, keep it outdoors only where prolonged cold is uncommon; colder areas can grow it in a container and move it to a bright shelter before frost.

Choose a site with room around the canopy and protection from strong wind or roof runoff. If the tree makes leaves but few flowers, check light, crowding, and persistently wet soil before adding fertilizer.

## Water by soil condition and crop stage

Water when the top layer begins to dry, wetting the root area and allowing drainage. Newly planted trees need closer checks, while established trees can tolerate a short dry interval. During flowering and fruit development, avoid repeated drying because young fruit may drop or size unevenly.

After heavy rain, reduce watering and clear runoff from the base. A thin organic mulch layer steadies moisture, but leave open space around the trunk so bark does not stay damp.

## Use loose soil and feed modestly

Loose, organic-rich, well-drained soil supports healthy roots. Mix mature compost or well-rotted organic matter at planting, then apply a thin layer around the canopy edge while the tree is making leaves and fruit. If leaves pale or new branches are weak, use a diluted liquid feed according to its label.

Keep concentrated fertilizer away from the trunk and never feed dry soil. Excess nitrogen makes a dense canopy, less fragrant fruit, and harder inspection of leaf undersides. Watch new shoots, flowers, and soil moisture before adding more.

## Plant a healthy tree and propagate deliberately

A grafted, layered, or clearly sourced young tree makes fruit traits easier to predict than an uncontrolled seedling. When transplanting, keep the root ball intact and set it at the same soil level as before. Water gently after planting and provide light protection from harsh sun if the roots were disturbed.

Seed can still produce a new tree, but seedlings may differ from the parent in size, aroma, and fruiting time. Clear weeds around the trunk so a young tree does not compete for water and nutrients.

## Prune branches and harvest fragrant fruit

After each crop, remove dead, inward-growing, and crossing branches so light reaches the canopy. New growth after pruning can carry flowers, so do not cut the whole tree back at once. Lower branches that are too high to inspect or harvest safely.

Fruit usually becomes more fragrant and slightly softer as it ripens. Skin color varies by variety, so do not use color as the only sign. Cut the stem with scissors, support the fruit, and harvest in batches. Firm fruit can continue ripening in a clean, airy place.

## Check early for pests and disease

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) may gather on young shoots, curling leaves and leaving sticky residue. [Mealybugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/mealybugs) form white clusters in branch joints, while [whiteflies](richfarm://pests-diseases/whiteflies) fly up when leaves are disturbed. Inspect new growth and leaf undersides; wipe, rinse, or prune a small colony when caught early.

[Leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot) causes brown or dark marks that spread across leaves. If the tree wilts while soil is wet, roots are brown and mushy, or the base is soft, improve drainage to reduce possible [root rot](richfarm://pests-diseases/root_rot). Open the canopy, avoid wetting leaves, and identify the cause before using a product.

## Light and location

Eggplants are sun-loving plants that need at least 6 hours of direct sunlight a day to flower and set fruit. A sunny spot keeps the plant healthy, colors the fruit evenly, and limits disease. In shade, plants grow leafy but set few fruit.

The plant grows as an upright bush with a fairly sturdy stem, but heavy fruit can pull branches down. Stake the main stem loosely once the fruit begins to enlarge; this keeps the fruit off the ground and makes picking easier. No trellis is needed, since this is not a climbing plant.

## Watering

Keep the soil evenly moist, especially from flowering until the fruit reaches full size. Water when the top layer begins to dry, soaking around the base, then let the soil drain. Long dry spells make flowers drop and young fruit stop growing; soil kept soggy invites root rot.

On hot days, potted plants dry faster than garden plants and need checking more often. Water at the base rather than over the leaves, especially late in the day.

## Soil

Choose loose, fertile soil with good drainage. Work mature compost or well-rotted manure into the bed before planting. In containers, use a large pot since the roots spread widely; keep the drainage holes clear and never leave the pot standing in water.

## Feeding

Prepare the soil before planting, then feed lightly once flowering begins. During fruit development, favor a fertilizer with more potassium than nitrogen for firm fruit. Watch the leaves before adding more: pale leaves and slow growth despite enough water may signal a nutrient shortage, while dark leaves and thick stems mean no extra feeding is needed.

Keep concentrated fertilizer away from the stem and do not feed while the soil is dry. With liquid products, dilute as directed rather than increasing the dose.

## Sowing and propagation

Eggplants germinate and grow more slowly than many other vegetables, so in cool regions start seed indoors about 8-10 weeks before planting out. Seed needs warm soil to germinate; keep the medium evenly moist but not waterlogged. In warm climates, direct sowing works once the weather has warmed for good.

Move seedlings into pots or prepared beds once they have several true leaves. Do not crowd them, since the canopy spreads and needs airflow. After transplanting, water well and provide light shade for the first few days if the sun is intense.

## Ongoing care and harvest

The plant flowers continuously, and each pollinated flower produces one fruit. While the fruit is small, remove some from weak branches so the plant concentrates on the rest. Keep the canopy open by removing old lower leaves, letting sunlight and air reach the inside.

Fruit is ready when it reaches the variety's size, with tight, glossy skin that still feels soft. Left too long, the skin hardens, the seeds enlarge, and the flavor turns bitter while the plant stops setting new fruit. Cut the stem with scissors rather than pulling, so the branch is not damaged. Harvesting regularly encourages another round of fruit.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young shoots and leaf undersides, curling leaves and slowing growth. In hot, dry weather, [spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) may appear as fine stippling on the leaves with thin webbing underneath. Check the undersides regularly; a small number can be washed off or removed by hand.

[Leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot) can cause unusual spots that spread in damp weather. Remove affected leaves, avoid wetting the foliage, and keep the planting area open. If several plants wilt while the soil is still wet, check the drainage first, since waterlogged roots can also cause wilting; if the problem persists, identify the exact cause before treating anything.

## Light and location

Common beans are sun-loving plants that grow best in full sun, about 6 hours of direct light a day. Plenty of sun gives even flowering and limits moisture-related disease. In hot climates, sow in the cool season or choose a spot with afternoon shade so the plants do not overheat.

There are two main types to tell apart before planting: bush beans, which are low and stand on their own without support, and pole beans, which twine and need a trellis or stakes. For pole beans, make the support a comfortable picking height; without it the vines sprawl on the ground and become hard to manage.

## Watering

Keep the soil evenly moist, especially from flowering through pod set. Water when the top layer begins to dry; dry soil at flowering makes flowers and young pods drop. Avoid overwatering to the point of sogginess, since bean roots are easily damaged by standing water.

Water at the base and limit wetting the leaves. Leaves that stay damp invite fungal disease, so watering in the morning so the foliage dries during the day also helps.

## Soil

Beans are not fussy about soil but do best in loose, well-drained soil rich in organic matter. Work compost into the bed before sowing to keep the soil soft and nourished. Avoid heavy soil that holds water, which weakens the roots.

## Feeding

Beans belong to a group that forms its own nitrogen with the help of root bacteria, so they need much less nitrogen than many other vegetables. A light feed of well-rotted organic fertilizer worked in before sowing is enough for most crops. If the leaves look pale and growth is slow, feed lightly as directed.

Avoid heavy nitrogen, which produces plenty of leaves but few flowers and pods. Keep concentrated fertilizer away from the stem and do not feed while the soil is dry.

## Sowing and propagation

Beans are usually sown directly where they will grow, since the roots dislike disturbance from transplanting. Sow once the soil has warmed and frost is no longer a risk; seed germinates quickly in warm soil. Plant 2-3 seeds per spot and thin to the strongest seedling.

Bush types are sown in rows and need no stakes; pole types are sown at the base of a trellis or stakes so the vines can twine as they grow. In cool regions, sow after the last frost; in warm climates, sow successive batches a few weeks apart to extend the harvest.

## Ongoing care and harvest

For pole types, guide the young vines gently onto the trellis as they lengthen; they will twine around the support on their own. Keep the canopy open and weed around the base so the plants are not competing for water and nutrients. Avoid working among the plants while the leaves are wet with dew, since this spreads disease.

Young pods are often ready to pick only a few weeks after the flowers open, depending on the variety and weather. Pick while they are young, tender, and before the seeds swell visibly in the pod; older pods turn fibrous and signal the plant to stop producing. Hold the branch and snap the pod off gently, or cut the stem with scissors, to avoid injuring the vine. Regular picking keeps the plant producing.

## Common pests and diseases

[Aphids](richfarm://pests-diseases/aphids) gather on young shoots and leaf undersides, curling leaves and distorting new growth. Check the tips while watering; a light infestation can be washed off or removed by hand. In hot, dry weather, [spider mites](richfarm://pests-diseases/spider_mites) may appear, pale-stippling the leaves and leaving fine webbing.

In damp weather, [rust](richfarm://pests-diseases/rust) can form orange rust-colored spots on the leaves; remove affected leaves and avoid wetting the foliage. [Leaf spot](richfarm://pests-diseases/leaf_spot) can also spread when leaves stay wet; keep the planting area open and do not work near the plants while they are wet. If disease spreads widely, identify the cause before using any product.

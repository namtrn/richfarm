## Light and location

Aloe vera is a succulent that loves bright light and grows well in strong indirect light or a few hours of gentle direct sun a day. Enough light keeps the leaves thick and upright; too little light makes the plant stretch, with thin, pale leaves. Full blazing sun all day can turn the leaves brown or scorch them, especially on plants not used to it.

The plant forms a dense rosette of fleshy leaves from the base, with no tall stem, so it needs no support. Young offsets grow around the mother plant; once crowded, they can be separated and potted on their own. A terracotta pot or any container with good drainage suits it best.

## Watering

Aloe tolerates drought very well and waterlogging very poorly; overwatering is the most common cause of rot. Water only when the soil has dried completely, usually when the top layer and a few centimeters below are dry. Soak it, then let the soil dry fully before watering again.

In the cold season or in low light, the plant needs very little water, sometimes only every few weeks. Do not leave water pooled in the leaf rosette, since this causes rot. If the leaves look wrinkled or soft, check the soil: wrinkles often mean prolonged dryness, while soft, mushy leaves usually mean too much water.

## Soil

Use a very well-draining mix made for succulents, or potting soil amended with coarse sand, perlite, or pumice. The pot needs clear drainage holes and should never be left standing in water after watering. Soil that holds water for long is the main cause of root rot.

Indoors, place the pot near a bright window and rotate it weekly so all sides receive more even light.

## Feeding

Aloe needs little fertilizer. A diluted liquid fertilizer applied as directed every few months during the growing season is enough; stop feeding in the cold season. Do not feed while the soil is dry, and avoid concentrated doses, since succulent roots are easily damaged.

## Sowing and propagation

The most common way to propagate aloe is by separating the offsets that grow around the mother plant. Gently lift an offset that has its own roots, let the cut dry for a few days, then pot it in well-draining mix. Keep the mix slightly moist until the offset roots, then water as you would a mature plant.

Growing from seed is less common because it is slower and harder. When repotting, choose a slightly larger pot with fresh mix; repot at the start of the growing season so the plant recovers quickly.

## Ongoing care and harvest

Wipe dust off the leaves regularly with a damp cloth so they photosynthesize well. Rotate the pot so the plant grows evenly on all sides if one side gets more sun. Remove old or damaged leaves near the base to keep the plant tidy and avoid pests.

When you need the leaf gel, choose an outer, mature, thick leaf and cut it near the base with a clean knife. Use the cut leaf right away or store it in the refrigerator for a short time. Do not remove too many leaves at once, since the plant needs them to photosynthesize and keep growing.

## Common pests and diseases

[Mealybugs](richfarm://pests-diseases/mealybugs) often cluster at the leaf bases and in the leaf axils as white cottony spots; wipe them off with a cotton swab or damp cloth when you first notice them. Check the leaf bases regularly, since mealybugs hide easily in the sheltered parts.

Overwatering causes [root rot](richfarm://pests-diseases/root_rot): the leaves turn soft and mushy, yellow-brown, and the base smells musty. If this happens, stop watering, lift the plant from the pot, cut away the damaged roots, and repot into fresh, well-draining mix. Do not water again until the soil has dried completely.
